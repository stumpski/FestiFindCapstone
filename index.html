THE ABSTRACT

Large music festivals and crowded events present a serious safety challenge: existing location-tracking technologies fail precisely when they are needed most.

While tools like Apple AirTag, FindMy, and Life360 dominate the consumer market, no research or product has successfully addressed the accuracy degradation these systems experience in dense, signal-saturated crowds leaving festival-goers, particularly women and LGBTQ+ individuals who face disproportionate rates of harassment and harm, without a reliable way to stay connected to their trusted group.

FestiFind fills this gap by proposing a wearable Bluetooth Low Energy tracker small enough to attach to a Kandi rave bracelet — a staple of the rave community — using an anchor-tag triangulation mechanism that does not rely on cellular service and is not degraded by physical crowd density.

To develop this concept, I researched existing BLE positioning technologies including u-blox XPLR-AOA anchor systems and the DA14531 chip module, consulted directly with the engineering team at Totem Compass (the closest existing competitor), and explored grant funding pathways through UMD SPIN to finance a working prototype.

FestiFind is designed specifically to serve vulnerable populations at large events offering an affordable, culturally embedded safety tool that festivals themselves could adopt and promote, creating mutual benefit for organizers and attendees alike.

Keeping people connected to their people while enjoying music shouldn't cost $80 or require carrying around a massive hockey puck.

✦ Explanation of Deliverable

The diagram to the right details how the technology behind FestiFind works. Essentially, it relies on two physical components: an anchor and tag. The tag is what the user will wear on a rave bracelet as an attached charm as you can see in examples below. The tags in the diagram (DA14531 chips) are small enough to attach to a rave bracelet without becoming a burden to users which is what I had in mind when researching location tracking technologies. The second physical component required for FestiFind to work is the anchor which are the green boards in the diagram. The way in which these two components work together is by using the angle of arrival and departure from the tags to the anchors. The anchors then use those angles to triangulate the position of the tags.

For FestiFind to become a reality, we will have to partner with various festivals and events to place these anchors around their grounds to maintain location accuracy. Once these anchors are in place, tags can be connected to them using Bluetooth Low Energy technology and then that location can be sent to a customer application similar to what FindMy and Life360 attempts to do. Once these tags, the anchors, and the user interface are programmed, the tags can be sent out to customers with instructions on how to connect them with the interface and anchors can be sent out to festivals.

Explication (200–400+ words)

Starting with the initial research, I used websites like Totem Labs and articles like A Tale of Three Location trackers to gain some introductory insight into what location trackers already exist and how they work. With these sources brought some initial challenges such as figuring out what opportunities there were for me to innovate upon. From these I found that current trackers are too large, expensive, and are hindered in large crowds due to cellular service issues. Moving on from this initial research I then used videos like No Signal, No Problem, and documentation like the Bluetooth Direction Finding User Guide after being pointed to technology I could use to build a prototype.

After mapping out what my product would look like, I did some risk assessment on the impact of my product with sources like Sexual Harassment and Violence at Music Concerts and Festivals as well as Airtags and Unwanted surveillance to see what negative impacts my product might bring with its tracking capabilities. Lastly, I used the SPIN network from UMD for grant research to get funding for my prototype and websites for music festivals to see who I could pitch my idea to if I eventually need to collaborate with festival organizers.

Overall, I tended to rely on more practical sources as opposed to academic articles as innovating upon the theory and math for location tracking is not something within the scope of a semester long projects. I made great use of the documentation for anchor and tags as well as other competitors in the field of tag trackers. The most useful source for this project was the engineers from Totem Labs and I would likely have been stuck without a product design without their guidance.

Prompt 01

What did this experience teach you about conceptualizing and designing a project from scratch? What takeaways will you carry into your next project?

Working on FestiFind taught me that the hardest part of designing a project from scratch isn't the building, its the scoping. Early on, I had a broad vision of what I wanted to create, but I quickly learned that a good idea means nothing without a concrete understanding of the constraints around it. Researching the existing technology, reaching out to Totem Compass, and digging into Bluetooth Low Energy documentation forced me to confront the gap between what I imagined and what was actually feasible with the time and resources I had.

One of the biggest takeaways is the value of talking to people who have already tried to solve your problem. The conversation with Totem Compass's engineering team saved me weeks of going down the wrong path and pointed me directly to the materials and architecture I needed. I will carry that instinct into every future project: to reach out early, ask specific questions, and treat my competitors as a resource just as valuable as academic sources. I also learned that a project does not need to be finished to be valuable. FestiFind does not have a working prototype yet, but the research, the grant applications, the technical blueprint, and the pitch materials are all real, actionable outputs. Moving forward I will think less about a project as something that ends with a finished product, and more as something that can end when you have laid a clear foundation to build off of.

Prompt 02

How did you utilize feedback from classmates and your instructor? What changed as a result?

The most useful feedback I received throughout this project came from my classmates and instructor pushing me to be more realistic about what my final deliverable could actually be. Early on, I was framing FestiFind as a project that would end with a physical working prototype, which was never really achievable given that I do not currently have the funds to purchase the hardware I would need.

The feedback I got redirected me toward treating this as a proof of concept and funding project instead, which honestly made the whole thing feel more real. As a result of that pivot, I shifted a significant portion of my energy toward researching grants and funding opportunities through tools like UMD SPIN, identifying sources that apply to student innovators and small technology projects.

Prompt 03

What kind of labor went into this project, and how do you feel about that work? What are you most proud of about the final product?

The effort that went into FestiFind was way more varied than I expected. Starting with the initial research I was going through articles, watching YouTube series on ESP32 systems, and reading through Bluetooth Low Energy documentation which was honestly pretty dense but started making sense the more I sat with it. Then there was the outreach side: writing a cold email to an actual company, figuring out what to even ask them, and then getting a real response back from Totem Compass's engineering team that actually pointed me in a useful direction.

Beyond the technical stuff I also put a lot of time into the risk assessment side of things, looking at sources on sexual harassment at festivals and the surveillance concerns around products like AirTags to make sure I was thinking about the potential downsides of what I was building. Then there was the grant research through UMD SPIN and looking into festival organizers I could eventually pitch to, which was its own whole process.

What I am most proud of though is presenting to my classmates. Being able to explain FestiFind and my work to people who had no context for what I was building, and actually having people understand the problem and get why my solution made sense felt very satisfying. That's what it made it feel less like a class project and more like a real product.
